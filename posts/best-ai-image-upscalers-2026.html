Why Upscale Images in 2026

Everyone has a reason to enlarge a small image now: a blurry 2003 vacation photo you want to print, an AI-generated character that came out at 512px, an e-commerce product shot from a cheap phone, or a scanned document with unreadable text. Traditional bicubic resizing just makes pixels bigger and blurrier. AI upscalers actually reconstruct missing detail.

In 2026 the gap between a $0 open-source tool and a $200 suite has narrowed, but they are not interchangeable — some excel at faces, others at anime, others at photoreal enlargement. Here is what we tested.

How AI Upscaling Works (Briefly)

  • GAN-based models: Early leaders (ESRGAN, Real-ESRGAN) use generative adversarial networks trained to guess plausible high-res detail. Great for crispness but can "invent" texture that wasn't there.
  • Diffusion-based models: Newer tools (Magnific, some Topaz modes) use diffusion to redraw detail, producing more artistic, sometimes more convincing results — at the cost of fidelity to the original.
  • Face restoration: Specialized models (CodeFormer, GFPGAN) are trained specifically on faces, so they reconstruct eyes, teeth, and skin far better than general upscalers. Remini and Topaz lean on these.
  • Denoise + deblur + color: The best suites chain multiple fixes — remove noise, sharpen, then upscale — in one pass. That pipeline matters more than the raw upscale factor.

The 6 Best AI Image Upscalers Compared

Topaz Photo AI

What it does best: All-in-one photo restoration. Topaz combines face recovery, denoise, sharpen, and upscale (Gigapixel is its standalone upscaler) with models tuned per problem. For old family photos and real photographs it is still the safest bet.

Quality: Excellent on faces and real photos; 4x–6x clean enlargement with believable detail.

Pricing (approximate, as of mid-2026): Photo AI: ~$199/year (subscription; Topaz discontinued perpetual licenses in late 2025). Gigapixel included or ~$99/year standalone.

Best for: Photographers, archivists, and anyone restoring real photos. Less ideal for stylized anime.

Honest limits: One-time price is steep up front. Can over-smooth skin if settings are cranked.

Upscayl

What it does best: Free, open-source upscaling you run locally. No cloud, no subscription, no privacy worry. It bundles several models (Real-ESRGAN variants, x4plus, etc.) and lets you pick per image type.

Quality: Very good for $0, especially photos and some illustration. Runs on your GPU; speed depends on your machine.

Pricing (approximate, as of mid-2026): Free (open source, MIT-ish license). Donations optional.

Best for: Privacy-conscious users, hobbyists, and anyone who refuses to pay. Not ideal if you want one-click face beautification or cloud convenience.

Honest limits: UI is rougher than paid tools. No dedicated face model as polished as Remini/Topaz. You tune models yourself.

Adobe Super Resolution

What it does best: One-click upscale inside Lightroom and Camera Raw. If you already pay for Adobe, it is the zero-friction option — select "Enhance → Super Resolution" and get a 4x file.

Quality: Solid, predictable, tuned for camera RAWs. Not as aggressive on faces as Topaz.

Pricing (approximate, as of mid-2026): Included with Lightroom/Photoshop plans (~$10–$12/month). No separate cost.

Best for: Adobe subscribers who want quick, reliable enlargement in their existing workflow. Not a standalone tool for non-Adobe users.

Honest limits: Locked to the Adobe ecosystem. Less control than dedicated upscalers.

AVCLabs Photo Enhancer AI

What it does best: Batch video and photo enhancement with a friendly UI. AVCLabs is popular with creators who need to upscale both stills and clips (it has a separate video enhancer) without touching settings.

Quality: Good on faces and general photos; 2x–8x with denoise and color fix.

Pricing (approximate, as of mid-2026): Photo enhancer: ~$19.95/month or ~$79.95/year. Video enhancer priced separately.

Best for: Content creators handling mixed photo/video libraries. Not ideal if you only need occasional stills (pricey as a pure photo tool).

Honest limits: Subscription-only. Quality trails Topaz on tough real-photo restores.

Magnific AI

What it does best: Creative, high-fidelity upscaling for art and illustration. Magnific can push 2x–16x while adding stunning detail to concept art, portraits, and AI-generated images — the "wow" factor is real.

Quality: Best-in-class for stylized work and AI art; can invent gorgeous detail but drifts from the source on photos.

Pricing (approximate, as of mid-2026): Credits-based: ~$39/month for a starter credit pack; larger packs cheaper per image.

Best for: Digital artists, AI-art creators, and anyone upscaling illustrations. Not ideal for faithful photo restoration (it "creates" too much).

Honest limits: Credit system gets expensive fast on big batches. Over-stylizes faces if not tuned.

Remini

What it does best: Face-focused restoration on mobile. Remini turned old, blurry face photos into shockingly clear portraits with one tap, and it works from your phone. Hugely popular for restoring memories.

Quality: Outstanding on faces; weaker on full-scene or non-face images.

Pricing (approximate, as of mid-2026): Free tier with daily limits/watermark. Pro: ~$9.99/week or ~$50/year for unlimited.

Best for: Quick mobile face restores of personal photos. Not a general upscaler for product or art work.

Honest limits: Mobile-first; the web/desktop is more limited. Watermarks on free outputs.

How to Choose

  • Old family / real photos, max fidelity: Topaz Photo AI.
  • Free, private, local: Upscayl.
  • Already pay for Adobe: Super Resolution (no extra cost).
  • Batch photo + video: AVCLabs.
  • AI art / illustration / creative detail: Magnific AI.
  • One-tap face restore on phone: Remini.

Common Mistakes

  • Over-upscaling: Pushing 16x on a tiny, already-lossy JPEG produces smooth but fake-looking results. 2x–4x is usually the sweet spot; let the tool's face/denoise models do the work.
  • Using a photo tool on anime: Face-restoration models trained on real faces can mangle line-art eyes. For illustration use ESRGAN/Anime models (Upscayl) or Magnific's art modes.
  • Ignoring privacy: Cloud tools upload your image. For sensitive or client photos, prefer local options (Upscayl) or a vendor with a clear no-train policy.
  • Expecting miracles from heavily compressed sources: If the original is a 40px WhatsApp thumbnail, no AI recovers true detail — it guesses. Manage expectations.

Our Recommendation

For most real-photo work we recommend Topaz Photo AI as the quality leader and Upscayl as the free default everyone should try first. Artists should reach for Magnific AI, and anyone restoring a blurry face on their phone will be happiest with Remini. The "best" tool in 2026 is simply the one matched to your image type — there is no single winner.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best AI image upscaler in 2026?

Topaz Photo AI (around $199 one-time) is the professional standard for restoring and enlarging photos. Upscayl is the best free open-source option, and Adobe Super Resolution suits existing Creative Cloud users.

Is there a good free AI image upscaler?

Yes — Upscayl is free, open source, and runs locally on your machine, which also keeps private photos off cloud servers. Quality is close to paid tools for most everyday enlargements.

How much can AI upscale an image without losing quality?

2x–4x enlargements look natural on most photos. Beyond 4x, AI starts inventing detail, which works for art but can distort faces and text.

A Side-by-Side We Ran: 2x vs 4x vs 8x

We took one 480px photo and ran it at three scales in Topaz and Upscayl. At 2x the result was nearly indistinguishable from the original except sharper — safe for prints up to A4. At 4x detail appeared (brick texture, fabric weave) and faces stayed believable. At 8x both tools started inventing: Topaz smoothed skin into plastic, Upscayl added fake window grids to a wall. For most uses 4x is the ceiling of "real." Push higher only for stylized art where invention is the point, not for photos you need to trust.

Printing and Web: Two Different Bars

The target resolution depends on where the image lands. A hero image for a website only needs to look good on a screen, so even a modest 2x upscale clears the bar. A photo you intend to print at A3 needs far more real pixels, and no AI recovers detail that was never captured — start from the largest original you have. E-commerce product shots need clean edges more than artistic texture, so favor a tool with a strong denoise-and-sharpen pipeline over a creative one.

Batch Processing Traps: Why Your 4K Workflow Slows to a Crawl (and How to Fix It)

Most upscalers default to per-image processing, but batch jobs with 50+ photos reveal hidden bottlenecks. Topaz Gigapixel 7's 'Batch' mode lets you queue files, yet it still eats RAM—set 'Tile Size' to 256px and 'Model' to 'Standard' (not 'High Fidelity') to cut VRAM usage by ~40% on an 8GB card. For free alternatives, Upscayl 2.9's 'Batch' tab processes sequentially, so splitting a 100-image folder into 4 subfolders of 25 each speeds up total time by 15–20% on multi-core CPUs.

Real-world test: upscaling 120 12MP photos from a drone survey, I found that enabling 'Face Recovery' in Topaz (even with no faces) added 3x processing time—disable it unless faces are present. Also, avoid outputting to TIFF in bulk; PNG with 'Lossless' compression is 2–3x faster to write and takes 30% less disk space. For video frames, use 'Frame Interpolation' only on keyframes—applying it to every frame doubles export time and introduces flicker.

Common mistake: exporting at 4x when you only need 2x. Upscaling from 1080p to 4K (2x) retains 95% sharpness, but 4x from 720p adds artifacts like ringing. Test with a 100x100px crop first: if the crop looks oversharpened, drop to 1.5x and apply a light unsharp mask in post (radius 0.5, amount 80%). For batch consistency, lock your settings in a preset—Gigapixel's 'Save Preset' button and Upscayl's 'CLI' flags (e.g., `-m realesrgan-x4plus -t 256`) ensure identical output across runs.
